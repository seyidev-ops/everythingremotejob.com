Read Your Rejections Properly — They Tell You Where It Is Breaking

Where a search fails tells you what to fix. Four failure points, four different problems, and four different answers.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 27 June 2026 5 min read

"It is not working" is not a diagnosis. A job search breaks at one of four specific points, and each one means something different. Find yours before changing anything.

Break point 1 · No replies at all

Symptom: fifty applications, zero responses of any kind, not even automated rejections that mention you.

What it means: you are not reaching humans. This is almost always the CV — two columns, contact details in the header, no keywords matching the listing — or applying to roles that exclude your country.

Fix: stop applying entirely. Rebuild the CV as one column with achievements and numbers, run the free scan, and check the location line on every listing before you spend forty minutes on it. Sending more of the same is the most common and most expensive mistake in job hunting.

Break point 2 · Replies, but no first interview

Symptom: acknowledgements and polite rejections, no calls.

What it means: you are being read and passed over. Usually a proof problem — nothing shareable — or an experience-translation problem, where what you did is described in your employer's language rather than the market's.

Fix: build three portfolio pieces with real context. Rewrite your experience in the vocabulary of the listings you are targeting.

Break point 3 · First interviews, no second

Symptom: conversations happen, then nothing.

What it means: the interview itself. Usually unstructured answers, no evidence with numbers, no questions asked at the end, or a fumbled answer to the remote-logistics question.

Fix: write six situation-task-action-result stories and practise them out loud. Prepare the infrastructure and overlap answers with specifics. Record yourself once and watch it back.

Break point 4 · Final stages, no offer

Symptom: you reach the last two or three, repeatedly, and lose.

What it means: this is the good problem. You are competitive. It is usually fit, salary expectation, or being marginally behind someone with direct sector experience.

Fix: ask for feedback every time — at this stage people often give it. Bring a ninety-day plan. Check your stated range against the market. And keep going: at this break point, the numbers are already on your side.

The diagnostic habit

Keep a column in your tracker for how far each application got. After twenty, the pattern is obvious and it points at exactly one of the four fixes above.

Without that column, every rejection feels like the same undifferentiated failure — which is what makes people conclude the market is closed, when in fact one specific, fixable thing is breaking.

The email worth sending

"Thanks for letting me know, and for closing the loop. If you have thirty seconds: was there one thing that would have made me stronger? Genuinely trying to improve rather than argue the decision."

About one in four replies. That reply is frequently worth more than the interview was.
